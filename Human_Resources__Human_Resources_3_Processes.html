🔄 Human Resources 3 · Processes — The HR Cycle

Human Resources · ① Role · ② Influences · ③ Processes ← you are here · ④ Strategies · 📕 Full note

✅ HSC SCOPE — everything is examinable. The HSC on Monday 26 October covers the full module.

⭐ The HR cycle is the spine of this topic. Many questions are structured around one stage. Learn the four in order.

   ACQUISITION  →  DEVELOPMENT  →  MAINTENANCE  →  SEPARATION
   (get them)      (grow them)     (keep them)     (exit them)
        ↑                                               │
        └───────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                     (cycle repeats)

1. Acquisition

The stage that involves identifying staff needs, recruitment and selection.

Identifying needs: If there is a missing skill, or more work than current workers can do in a timely manner, the business may need to recruit.

Once the need is established, the business must write:

Document Definition
Job description Describes the tasks and responsibilities of the employee
Job specification Defines the skills, knowledge and abilities required for the job

1.1 Recruitment

Recruitment = where the business accumulates a pool of potential candidates for a job.

Method Detail
Internal Moving or promoting existing people
External Outsourcing to private employment agencies; interviews on university campuses; trainee positions through cadetships
Internal External
Advantages Cheaper, faster, motivates staff, known performance, shorter induction Fresh ideas, larger talent pool, new perspectives, brings in scarce skills
Disadvantages Limited pool, creates another vacancy, risk of "inbreeding" of ideas Expensive, unknown performance, longer adjustment and induction

1.2 Selection

Selection = a screening process in staff acquisition. Information gathered about applicants is reviewed and the most appropriate applicant is chosen.

Information is gathered through:

  • Interviews — one-to-one or panel
  • Application forms
  • Referee checks
  • Written references
  • Written / computer tests

Case study — Apple's recruitment What Apple looks for: cross-collaboration, flexibility and adaptability to new things, problem solving and thinking outside the box, ability to keep up with a fast pace of work. Apple's selection process: typically 2–3 interviews and potentially up to 7. Candidates are observed from the start for personality and behaviour; there are introductions and small conversations, candidates are split into sections and placed in scenarios to demonstrate their responses, and asked questions throughout.

1.3 Induction Training

Induction is the transition from acquisition to development in the HR cycle. It is the procedure where a new employee is introduced to the business and becomes familiar with how it works.

What induction must include:

  • Shown all policies and procedures
  • Introduced to co-workers and allocated a mentor
  • Understanding of work health and safety procedures, including emergency procedures
  • Shown where facilities are
  • Entitlements — how breaks work, how long and how often

Why induction is necessary for all new employees, even experienced ones: Policies and procedures differ between businesses. Even an experienced worker needs to know this business's rules, be introduced to co-workers and a mentor, know what to do in an emergency, know where everything is, and understand their entitlements. It gets employees to a work-ready state faster and saves them the time of finding everything out themselves.

2. Development

Businesses must develop the skills and capabilities of staff to maintain competitive advantage. Development involves preparing employees for future responsibilities due to a change in the business's strategies or growth in its size or market share.

Effective development results in increased motivation, greater business flexibility, and improved use of technology and innovation.

Development includes training and performance appraisals.

2.1 Training

Training = any activity aimed at improving an employee's present and future performance in the workforce.

Method Examples
On the job Apprenticeships, mentoring, coaching, job rotation, shadowing
Off the job External conferences, TAFE courses, university, workshops

Benefits of training and development:

For the employee For the business
Enhanced skills More efficient
More marketable More productive
Better job satisfaction
For the economy For the customer
More competitive Gets a better service/product
More productive

On the job vs off the job:

Advantages Disadvantages
On the job Generally the most cost effective; employees remain productive; opportunity to learn whilst doing; training alongside real colleagues Quality depends on the trainer's ability and available time; bad habits may be passed on; learning environment may not be conducive; potential disruption to operations
Off the job A wider range of skills or qualifications can be obtained; can learn from outside specialists and experts; employees more confident when starting a job More expensive; lost working time and output; employees with new qualifications may leave for better jobs

2.2 Performance Appraisal

Performance appraisal = a formal assessment of how well a person is working. It identifies:

  • The person's strengths
  • Areas that need further development
  • Areas the employee would like further professional development in
  • The professional goals of the employee

This forms the basis for promotions and pay rises.

Why it must be managed constructively: A poorly handled appraisal demotivates, damages trust and increases turnover. A constructive one clarifies expectations, identifies development needs and motivates.

Why appraisals help employers assess HR effectiveness: Aggregated appraisal data reveals whether training is working, where skill gaps remain, and whether recruitment is selecting the right people.

Other performance management methods: KPIs, 360-degree feedback (supervisor, peers, subordinates, self), balanced scorecard (financial, customer, internal processes, learning and growth).

3. Maintenance

Maintenance is the stage where owners and managers ensure existing staff are:

  • Kept motivated
  • Remain loyal
  • Retained

Staff are maintained by providing working conditions and a work environment that motivate them to be increasingly productive and gain satisfaction from their work.

3.1 Benefits Used to Maintain Staff

Type Examples
Monetary benefits — paid on top of usual entitlements, forming part of the remuneration package Commission; piece rate based on output; bonuses for meeting targets; shared ownership schemes; fringe benefits (e.g. a company car)
Non-monetary benefits Greater job variety; more flexible working hours; increased status in the job or community; autonomy in work; access to a subsidised cafeteria
Intrinsic rewards A reward that comes from within the person — in employment, the feeling of satisfaction that comes with doing a job well

3.2 Other Maintenance Strategies

Strategy Benefit to employer Benefit to employee
Employee participation Better decisions, higher commitment, lower turnover Sense of ownership, voice in decisions, higher satisfaction
Team work Improved problem solving, flexibility, peer support Social belonging, shared load, skill sharing
Flexible working conditions Wider talent pool, reduced absenteeism, lower office costs Work–life balance, reduced stress, retention of carers
Training and rewards Higher productivity, skilled workforce, competitive advantage Career progression, marketability, recognition

Flexible work practices: part-time, job sharing (two employees share one full-time role), telecommuting, flextime (flexible start/finish with core hours), compressed working week (full hours in fewer days).

Family-friendly programs: parental leave, carer's leave, on-site childcare, flexible hours. Likely benefits to the employer: improved retention of experienced staff, reduced absenteeism, stronger employer brand, wider recruitment pool.

Workplace diversity

  • Benefits: wider talent pool, better decision-making, improved creativity, better understanding of diverse customers, enhanced reputation
  • Challenges: communication barriers, potential bias, differing work styles, training costs, resistance to change

The cost of sexual harassment and bullying: legal liability and compensation, WHS breaches, absenteeism and stress leave, high turnover, low morale and productivity, reputational damage, difficulty recruiting.

4. Separation

Separation is the ending of the employment relationship. It may be initiated by either the employee or the employer, and is either voluntary or involuntary.

Type Forms
Voluntary Retirement; resignation; voluntary redundancy — where a job is no longer required, or the firm is restructuring or has been taken over
Involuntary — management decides employees are no longer required Involuntary redundancy — due to firms closing down or new technology (the Employment Protection Act 1982 (NSW) provides a minimum scale for severance payments for permanent employees under NSW awards, for employers with more than 15 employees); instant dismissal for employees who breach the law; summary dismissal for poor work performance

Why involuntary separation must be managed carefully: risk of unfair dismissal claims, damage to the morale of remaining staff ("survivor syndrome"), reputational damage, loss of corporate knowledge, and legal obligations around notice, consultation and severance.

Entitlements on redundancy: notice period (or payment in lieu), redundancy/severance pay based on years of service, accrued annual leave, accrued long service leave where applicable.

Role of the Fair Work Commission in unfair dismissal: receives and conciliates claims, determines whether the dismissal was harsh, unjust or unreasonable, and can order reinstatement or compensation.

Case study — NAB NAB announced plans to downsize its workforce by 12% to cut operating costs as more processes became automated. The bank planned to shed 6,000 roles while adding 2,000 technology jobs over three years, including specialists in software engineering, data and security. Hiring more tech professionals brings its own challenge because they are highly sought after.

Use this for: technology as an influence on HR; involuntary redundancy; the changing nature of skills; the tension between cost control (finance) and workforce capability (HR).

⚠️ Confusing pairs in this section

Pair The tell
Job description vs job specification Description describes the JOB. Specification specifies the PERSON. Mnemonic: deScription = the poSition; specification = the special qualities of the person
Recruitment vs selection Recruitment accumulates the pool. Selection chooses from it
Induction vs training Induction introduces them to this business. Training improves job performance
Training vs development Training is about now (current role). Development is about next (future responsibilities)
Maintenance vs development Maintenance keeps them. Development grows them
Redundancy vs dismissal Redundancy = the JOB no longer exists (restructuring, technology, closure) — not about performance, and it attracts severance pay. Dismissal = the PERSON is removed (misconduct or poor performance); the job usually still exists
Monetary / non-monetary / intrinsic Monetary = money or money's worth, paid by the business. Non-monetary = a benefit that isn't cash. Intrinsic = comes from inside the person — the business can create conditions for it but cannot hand it over
Piece rate vs commission Piece rate is based on output produced. Commission is based on sales made
Voluntary vs involuntary separation Voluntary = the employee chose. Involuntary = management decided. Note redundancy can be either
