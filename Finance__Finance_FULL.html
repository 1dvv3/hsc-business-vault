1. Role of Financial Management

Definition: Financial management deals with the analysis, interpretation and evaluation of all financial records. It is the financial manager's responsibility to source finance that enables the business to achieve its strategic goals.

1.1 Strategic Role of Financial Management

  • The process of setting objectives throughout the business and deciding what resources are needed to achieve them
  • Developing a strategic plan as part of financial management ensures the business survives and grows in a competitive world
  • Financial management provides a framework for all other business activities — it does not follow the lead of other managers

Exam trap: A common multiple-choice question asks what the strategic role of finance is. The answer is "provides a framework for all other business activities" — not "sets short-term goals" (that's tactical/operational).

1.2 Objectives of Financial Management

Five objectives, split by time horizon.

Horizon Objective Definition
Short term (within 12 months) Liquidity The ability to pay back current liabilities with current assets
Short term Profitability The ability of the business to maximise profits
Short term Efficiency The ability to manage costs and the collection of money from customers
Long term (2–5 years) Growth The ability of the business to grow and expand
Long term Solvency How the business is financed (debt vs equity) — the ability to meet long-term financial commitments

Confusing pair — Liquidity vs Solvency Liquidity = short-term. Can we pay bills due now with assets we can convert to cash now? Measured by the current ratio. Solvency = long-term. Can we meet commitments over years? Measured by the gearing ratio. Both are about paying debts — the difference is purely the time frame.

Long-term goal of the business is to increase the wealth of the owners. Long-term goals depend on profitability in the short term resulting from increased operating efficiency.

1.3 Interdependence with Other Key Business Functions

Interdependence = the mutual reliance between finance and the other key business functions.

Function Relationship with Finance
Operations Finance provides funding and budgets so products can be made profitably. Operations produces quality products with minimal expense, generating higher revenue margins that flow back to finance
Marketing Finance sets the marketing budget. Marketing generates the sales revenue that finance then allocates, monitors and reinvests
Human Resources Wages and training are major budget items. Finance sets salary budgets; HR must justify expenditure through productivity gains. Commission and incentive schemes are a joint HR–finance decision

Model "Explain" answer (scaffold from class) Define — Financial management is interdependent with operations because of the relationship between business revenue and expenditure. Relationship — Finance provides funding and budgets so the product can be produced profitably, while operations produces quality products with minimal expense to generate a higher revenue margin. Implications — Both functions supply each other with information and resources that drive growth and profit maximisation. Link — Hence the business grows in the marketplace and increases revenue margins, leading to increased profitability.

2. Influences on Financial Management

2.1 Internal Sources of Finance

Internal sources are recorded under equity in the balance sheet. The money comes from the owners or from the business's own activities.

Source Definition
Owner's equity Funds contributed by owners or partners to establish and build the business
Retained profits Net profit that has been reinvested in the business. Added as equity — increases the owner's claim

Owner's Equity section of the Balance Sheet = Capital + Retained Profits

2.2 External Sources of Finance

Financed from external organisations — either debt or equity.

  • Debt = any money that has been borrowed. Short-term debt is repayable within 12 months; long-term is over 12 months.
  • Equity = attached to the ownership of shares in an incorporated business — the owner's financial claim on the assets.

Debt Finance — Short Term

Source Detail
Overdraft Flexibility to borrow from a bank at short notice through a cheque/current account. Interest must be paid. Used for working capital and claimable as a tax deduction. More flexible than a bank loan; assists with short-term liquidity issues
Commercial bill A written order for a loan amount guaranteed by the business's bank. Borrowed from businesses with surplus funds. Normally 30–180 days, usually for amounts over $100,000
Factoring Selling accounts receivable to a specialist factoring firm to create cash inflow. The factoring company takes over management and collection of the unpaid accounts and pays the seller the value minus a commission. Business receives up to 90% within 48 hours. The riskiest source because of unpaid debts — that risk is borne by the debt collection agency

Debt Finance — Long Term

Source Detail
Mortgage A loan repaid over a set number of years with interest, used to purchase land or property. The property becomes the security. If the business defaults, the bank takes and sells the property
Debenture Long-term debt acquired by offering a prospectus to the general public on the securities exchange. Set interest rate for a fixed time. More risky to the lender than a mortgage — not tied to specific property, so there is no security. Unlike shares, the level of profit does not affect the interest rate
Unsecured notes Usually issued by finance companies to gain funds. Higher interest rates reflecting greater risk. Also called bonds
Leasing Leasing non-current assets (cars, equipment) for payments to the owner. Reduces the cost of acquiring assets as full value need not be paid instantly. Lease payments are tax deductible — they appear under Expenses in the Profit and Loss Statement, reducing net profit and therefore company tax

Two types of lease:

  1. Operating lease — short periods, normally shorter than the life of the asset. The owner carries out maintenance.
  2. Financial lease — the lessor purchases the asset on behalf of the lessee. Runs for the lifetime of the asset.

Why lease rather than buy? Lease costs are tax deductible; flexibility if the asset is only needed for a short period; distributes the cost over time, which is better for cash flow.

Equity Financing

Equity financing comes from the owners. Equity reflects the owner's financial claim on the assets of the business.

Legal structure Term used
Sole trader / Partnership Owners' Equity
Public / Private companies Shareholders' Funds

Private equity — current owners invite people or organisations to invest. Normally proprietary companies (Pty Ltd). Shareholders do not necessarily have control, and there is no guarantee of dividends.

Public equity — companies listed on the ASX; anyone can invest.

Type Definition
Ordinary shares Issued on the ASX, with or without voting rights at the AGM
New share issues Shares sold for the first time on the public market — either an IPO (initial public offering) or a new offering if more finance is needed
Rights issue The company offers existing shareholders the chance to buy more shares
Placements Additional shares offered to specific institutions (venture capitalists) and specific investors
Share purchase plans An offer to existing shareholders to purchase more shares without brokerage fees, normally below normal prices. Requires ASIC permission
Equity — Advantages Equity — Disadvantages
No debt servicing costs such as interest Takes time
If the business makes a loss, no dividend needs to be paid (though this may worry shareholders into selling) Dilutes ownership

Financial Grants

Financial gifts from government to help businesses establish. Strict criteria: location, personal characteristics of the owner, type of industry, type of goods/services. Criteria commonly include Indigenous people, regional businesses, the IT industry, and businesses providing innovative or essential services.

The government is not paid back and gets no ownership. Its return comes through increased employment, growth and taxes.

2.3 Financial Institutions

Institution Role
Banks Accept deposits and provide loans; the largest source of funds for business
Investment banks Trade in money, securities and financial futures; arrange long-term finance for expansion; advise on foreign exchange cover, mergers (two businesses agree to combine, e.g. Westpac and St George) and takeovers (one business buys another, which ceases to exist); operate unit trusts; arrange overseas finance at cheaper interest rates. Example: Macquarie Bank
Finance and life insurance companies Non-bank financial intermediaries specialising in smaller commercial finance. Provide secured and unsecured loans. Policyholders pay a premium, which is lent to businesses; the company earns income from interest
Superannuation funds Provide funds to the corporate sector through investment of superannuation contributions
Unit trusts (mutual funds) Take funds from a large number of small investors and invest them in specific types of financial assets
Australian Securities Exchange (ASX) Market operator, clearing house and payment system facilitator. Oversees compliance with operating rules and promotes corporate governance among listed companies

Primary vs Secondary Market — a guaranteed exam distinction

Primary market Secondary market
What is traded New shares, issued for the first time Pre-owned (existing) securities
Who gets the money The company receives the proceeds The selling shareholder — the company receives nothing
Purpose Raise new capital Provide liquidity so investors can buy and sell easily

Because the secondary market provides liquidity, it makes investing in the primary market more attractive.

2.4 Influence of Government

Influence Detail
Monetary policy The RBA assists the federal government to achieve low inflation and economic growth. Securities and loans are bought and sold to put pressure on interest rates and alter the economic cycle. This impacts debt servicing costs — if interest rates rise, loan repayment costs rise
ASIC (Australian Securities and Investments Commission) Enforces and administers the Corporations Act; protects consumers in investments, life and general insurance, superannuation and banking (except lending). Aims to reduce fraud and unfair practices. Ensures companies adhere to the law, collects company information and makes it public
Company taxation Companies pay company tax on profits, paid before dividends are distributed to shareholders

Why company tax matters to financial management: Tax reduces net profit available for distribution and reinvestment. It also makes debt more attractive than equity, because interest payments are generally tax-deductible expenses while dividends are not.

2.5 Global Market Influences

Financial risks in global markets are greater than domestic ones, but such risk is necessary for business strategy.

Influence Definition
Interest rates Comparison of domestic interest rates to rates in other countries
Availability of funds The ease with which a business can access funds on the international financial market
Global economic outlook The projected changes to the level of economic growth throughout the world

Why are these "uncontrollable"? They are external macro-environmental factors existing independently of any one business. A single firm cannot change global interest rates or economic trends — it can only develop internal strategies to mitigate their impact.

Negative global outlook → funding: When the outlook is negative, lenders become risk-averse and restrict lending because they fear non-repayment. Finance becomes harder to obtain, and where available, carries a higher interest rate.

3. Processes of Financial Management

3.1 Planning and Implementing

Financial planning is responsible for both short-term and long-term planning. It includes:

  1. Determining the viability of projects
  2. Making decisions and projections for future liquidity and performance
  3. Setting financial goals, and determining and implementing strategies to achieve them

Financial needs: A new business must determine establishment costs and how to finance them (debt and/or equity). An existing business must determine the financing required for future plans.

Budgets

Budgets show the cash required for planned outlays for a particular period, and the cost of capital expenditure and associated expenses.

Budgets are used as:

  • A plan for achieving set outcomes
  • A means of establishing standards for monitoring

Budgets sit in both the planning process (giving departments and cost centres a plan to follow) and the implementation process (identifying variances and flagging areas to investigate).

Type of budget Covers
Operating budgets Main activities — sales, production, raw materials, labour
Project budgets Capital expenditure and research and development
Financial budgets Funds required to pay for inputs and anticipated inflows from future sales

Record Systems

Systems that ensure data recorded and information provided is accurate, reliable, efficient and accessible. Minimising errors is important to fulfil the legal requirement to provide information to shareholders and the ATO.

Most errors in computerised accounting systems occur through human error at data entry.

All public companies are legally required to have an audit every year. Auditors are qualified, independent, external people who check the records to ensure the accounts show a true and fair view.

Financial Risks

The risk that a business is unable to pay its debts as they fall due. Factors include:

  1. Theft of goods
  2. Not meeting sales targets
  3. Non-payment of accounts by accounts receivable
  4. Interest rate rises

Risk and borrowing: There is a direct relationship between borrowing and risk. Higher debt (gearing) increases fixed interest costs, reduces financial flexibility, and raises the risk of insolvency if sales targets are not met.

Financial Controls

Controls ensuring financial plans are implemented effectively and achieving set goals:

  1. Monitoring and controlling budgets — ongoing throughout the year
  2. Monitoring finances through the preparation of the cash flow statement, income statement and balance sheet — for most businesses, monthly
  3. Use of financial ratios to monitor performance

3.2 Debt vs Equity Financing

Debt Equity
Repayment Must be repaid No repayment obligation
Cost Interest — tax deductible Dividends — not tax deductible
Ownership Ownership retained Dilutes ownership
Risk to business Higher — fixed commitments regardless of profit Lower — no payment required if a loss is made
Effect on gearing Increases gearing Decreases gearing
Speed Generally faster Slower — takes time

3.3 The Matching Principle

Matching the term and source of finance to the use of finance.

  • Short-term finance (overdraft, commercial bill, factoring) should fund short-term needs — working capital, inventory, wages
  • Long-term finance (mortgage, debenture, shares, leasing) should fund long-term assets — property, plant, equipment

Why it matters: Using an overdraft to buy a factory means the debt falls due long before the asset generates a return, creating a liquidity crisis. Using a 20-year mortgage to buy stock means paying interest for two decades on inventory sold in a month.

Note — a second meaning. "Matching principle" is also an accounting term (see §3.6): revenue must be matched to the expenses incurred in earning it, in the same accounting period. Read the question carefully to see which is being asked.

3.4 The Three Financial Statements

Memorise these three structures — they are examinable directly.

Cash Flow Statement

Opening Cash Balance
+ Cash Receipts (inflows)
− Cash Payments (outflows)
= Closing Cash Balance

Records actual cash movements over a period. A cash flow budget, by contrast, is a forecast of expected future cash flows used to identify shortfalls in advance.

Income Statement (Revenue Statement / Profit and Loss)

        Sales
Less:   Sales Returns
   =    NET SALES

Less:   Cost of Goods Sold
   =    GROSS PROFIT

Less:   Expenses  (selling + administrative + financial)
   =    NET PROFIT

The whole statement is two subtractions. Sales − COGS = Gross Profit. Gross Profit − Expenses = Net Profit.

Cost of Goods Sold is itself a calculation:

COGS = Opening Stock + Purchases − Closing Stock

Why closing stock is subtracted: stock still sitting in the warehouse wasn't sold, so it isn't a cost of goods sold. It carries forward as next period's opening stock.

The three expense categories — you may be asked to classify these:

Category What goes in it
Selling Advertising, sales staff wages and commission, delivery to customers
Administrative Office wages, rent, electricity, insurance, stationery, depreciation
Financial Interest on loans, bank fees, discounts given, bad debts
Worked example — building a revenue statement from scratch

Given: Sales $620,000 | Sales returns $20,000 | Opening stock $60,000 | Purchases $310,000 | Closing stock $70,000 | Selling expenses $55,000 | Administrative expenses $50,000 | Financial expenses $15,000

Step Working Result
Net Sales 620,000 − 20,000 $600,000
COGS 60,000 + 310,000 − 70,000 $300,000
Gross Profit 600,000 − 300,000 $300,000
Total Expenses 55,000 + 50,000 + 15,000 $120,000
Net Profit 300,000 − 120,000 $180,000

Then the ratios follow straight off it:

  • Gross Profit Ratio = (300,000 ÷ 600,000) × 100 = 50%
  • Net Profit Ratio = (180,000 ÷ 600,000) × 100 = 30%
  • Expense Ratio = (120,000 ÷ 600,000) × 100 = 20%

Check: Gross Profit Ratio − Expense Ratio = Net Profit Ratio. 50 − 20 = 30 ✅ Use this to catch arithmetic errors in the exam in about five seconds.

Four traps in these calculations
Trap The fix
Using Sales instead of Net Sales as the denominator If the question gives sales returns, you must deduct them first. Every ratio then uses net sales
Adding closing stock in COGS It's minus. Opening plus, closing minus
Dividing profit ratios by Gross Profit The denominator for gross profit ratio, net profit ratio and expense ratio is always sales
Forgetting × 100 and the % sign These three are percentages. Current ratio and gearing are ratios written 2:1 — no percentage

Net Profit vs Return on Owners' Equity. Both use net profit on top. Net profit ratio divides by sales (how efficiently the business converts revenue into profit). ROE divides by owners' equity (what the owners get back on what they put in). Different question, same numerator.

Balance Sheet

Owners' Equity = Assets (Current + Non-Current) − Liabilities (Current + Non-Current)

Shows the financial position at a point in time — a snapshot. The income statement, by contrast, shows performance over a period.

Current Non-Current
Assets Cash, accounts receivable, inventory Property, plant, equipment, vehicles, intangibles (goodwill, patents)
Liabilities Overdraft, accounts payable, short-term loans Mortgage, debentures, long-term loans

Worked example — building a balance sheet A business has: Cash $20,000 | Accounts Receivable $40,000 | Inventory $50,000 | Equipment $200,000 | Accounts Payable $30,000 | Overdraft $20,000 | Mortgage $150,000

  • Current Assets = 20,000 + 40,000 + 50,000 = $110,000
  • Non-Current Assets = $200,000 → Total Assets = $310,000
  • Current Liabilities = 30,000 + 20,000 = $50,000
  • Non-Current Liabilities = $150,000 → Total Liabilities = $200,000
  • Owners' Equity = 310,000 − 200,000 = $110,000

3.5 Financial Ratios

These are the ratios your course requires, with the formulas and benchmarks as taught. Learn these exact forms.

# Ratio Formula Objective Benchmark
1 Current Ratio Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities Liquidity 2:1
2 Gearing Ratio Total Liabilities ÷ Owners' Equity Solvency 0.5:1 to 1:1 (i.e. up to 100%)
3 Gross Profit Ratio (Gross Profit ÷ Sales) × 100 Profitability No ideal — compare to previous periods and industry average
4 Net Profit Ratio (Net Profit ÷ Sales) × 100 Profitability No ideal — compare to previous periods and industry average
5 Return on Owners' Equity (Net Profit ÷ Owners' Equity) × 100 Profitability No ideal — compare to previous periods and industry average
6 Expense Ratio (Total Expenses ÷ Sales) × 100 Efficiency Lowest possible
7 Accounts Receivable Turnover Step 1: Sales ÷ Accounts Receivable → Step 2: 365 ÷ Step 1 answer Efficiency 30 days

Common error — the gearing formula. Gearing is Total Liabilities ÷ Owners' Equity, not non-current liabilities over total assets. Some textbooks use other variants; use the one above for this course.

Confusing pair — Gearing vs Leverage. They describe the same idea: the proportion of the business funded by debt rather than equity. "Highly geared" = "highly leveraged" = heavy reliance on borrowed funds → higher financial risk but potentially higher return to owners.

What each ratio tells you (say this in the exam, don't just calculate)

Ratio Plain-English meaning
Current Ratio For every $1 of current liabilities, how many $ of current assets the business has
Gearing Ratio For every $1 of equity, how much debt the business has
Gross Profit Ratio For every $1 of sales, how much is left after paying COGS
Net Profit Ratio For every $1 of sales, how much is left after paying all expenses
Return on Owners' Equity How much return owners get back from their investment
Expense Ratio For every $1 of sales, how much is spent on expenses
Accounts Receivable Turnover How many days it takes to collect money from customers

Worked Ratio Example — full set

Given: Sales $500,000 | COGS $300,000 | Total Expenses $120,000 | Current Assets $150,000 | Current Liabilities $75,000 | Total Liabilities $200,000 | Owners' Equity $250,000 | Accounts Receivable $41,000

Ratio Working Result Interpretation
Gross Profit 500,000 − 300,000 $200,000 —
Net Profit 200,000 − 120,000 $80,000 —
Current Ratio 150,000 ÷ 75,000 2:1 Meets the 2:1 benchmark — sound liquidity
Gearing 200,000 ÷ 250,000 0.8:1 Within the 0.5–1:1 range — acceptable solvency
Gross Profit Ratio (200,000 ÷ 500,000) × 100 40% 40c in every sales dollar remains after COGS
Net Profit Ratio (80,000 ÷ 500,000) × 100 16% 16c in every sales dollar is net profit
Return on Owners' Equity (80,000 ÷ 250,000) × 100 32% Owners earn 32c per dollar invested
Expense Ratio (120,000 ÷ 500,000) × 100 24% 24c of every sales dollar goes to expenses
AR Turnover Step 1: 500,000 ÷ 41,000 = 12.2 → Step 2: 365 ÷ 12.2 ≈ 30 days Meets the 30-day benchmark

3.6 Limitations of Financial Reports

Financial reports can be complicated to understand without a financial background and may not give a clear picture of the performance and position of the business.

Normalised Earnings

Earnings adjusted for the cyclical ups and downs of the economy. On the Profit and Loss Statement and Retained Profits on the Balance Sheet, earnings are adjusted to remove unusual or one-off influences.

Example: the sale of land or property where the business made a capital gain. Normalised earnings show a true reflection of earnings from operations.

Capitalising Expenses

The purchase price of a non-current asset is added to the cost of setting it up, and that total is used as the value of the asset in the balance sheet. The value is then written off as an expense in the income statement over the asset's useful life — this is depreciation.

Worked example — capitalising expenses

Purchased new equipment for $1,000,000:

Item Amount
Purchase price $1,000,000
Delivery costs $500
Installation costs $1,500
Training costs $5,000
Value of asset in Balance Sheet $1,007,000 (historical cost)

These costs would normally appear as expenses in the P&L, except where they relate to purchasing a non-current asset — then they are included in the asset's value on the Balance Sheet.

The abuse: Taking ordinary expenses and inappropriately capitalising them is illegal. It lowers reported expenses, inflates profit, and misleads stakeholders.

Valuing Assets

Businesses normally follow the historical cost concept — recording assets at purchase price. But real values change:

  1. Appreciation — inflation and market forces mean land and property tend to rise in value, so the business should revalue to show a true reflection
  2. Wear and tear — equipment loses value, so it should be devalued through depreciation

Increasing asset values: To increase the value of assets such as land and property, a business needs at least two independent valuations as evidence.

Worked example — straight-line depreciation Historical cost $1,000,000 | Useful lifetime 10 years | Disposal value $1,000

Straight-line depreciation = (Historical cost − Disposal value) ÷ Useful lifetime
                           = (1,000,000 − 1,000) ÷ 10
                           = 999,000 ÷ 10
                           = $99,900 per year
Income Statement (Expenses) Balance Sheet (Non-Current Assets)
Year 1 Depreciation $99,900 1,000,000 − 99,900 accumulated = $900,100
Year 2 Depreciation $99,900 1,000,000 − 199,800 accumulated = $800,200

Intangibles: Goodwill, trademarks, licences and patents are impossible to value accurately, which is itself a limitation.

Timing Issues

Financial reports cover a period, usually one year. If the business experiences seasonal fluctuations, the position may not be a true representation — and businesses may exploit this to appear more profitable.

The matching principle (accounting sense): Each transaction should be recorded at the time it actually occurs. Revenue must be matched to all expenses incurred in earning that revenue in a particular accounting period — even if cash has not been paid or received.

By recording some transactions outside the current financial year, they will not appear on the current reports.

Debt Repayments

Reports do not disclose specific information about some debt repayments.

Example: money owed to workers for annual leave or sick leave. It is difficult to predict when workers will take leave, so the business cannot recognise exactly when the debt will be paid.

Notes to the Financial Statements

The details and additional information left out of the main reporting documents (balance sheet, income statement). These normally appear in the appendices of Annual Reports and/or a Prospectus.

Limitation: Notes are not regulated by law as strictly as the financial report itself, so notes may in fact be used to mislead.

3.7 Ethical Issues in Financial Reporting

Accountants must display:

  • Integrity
  • Objectivity
  • Confidentiality
  • A high level of professional and technical ability

Financial managers and accountants therefore cannot be "creative" when recording transactions and preparing reports in order to make the business appear more profitable.

Standards: IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards) and AAS (Australian Accounting Standards). AAS selects the appropriate IFRS standards to adapt to the Australian market and adds specific requirements. Business accounts must be audited and monitored by independent organisations such as ASIC, which checks accounts and procedures to ensure a true and fair representation.

Unethical Financial Practices

Practice Definition
Audits (misuse of) Audits are independent examinations of financial information — internal or external. External auditors are independent; compromising that independence is unethical
Cut-off periods Financial reporting timed to give a misleading impression of current performance
Inappropriate use of funds Company officials using company assets for personal gain; false invoices issued for more than inputs are worth
Corporate raiding Buying an undervalued company and increasing profit or selling off the assets. The owner's personal wealth increases at the cost of employees who lose their jobs
Misuse of business credit cards Managers and employees using business credit cards and money for personal use

Other issues: fictitious revenues, hidden liabilities and expenses, improper disclosures or omissions, and fraudulent asset valuations.

Six Named Ethical Examples

Issue Explanation and example
Window dressing Using legal techniques to show a better financial position than actually exists. Example: at year end, offering a valued customer bulk stock at a discount. This shows improved cash flow and low stock on the Balance Sheet. Ethically it may not show a true and fair position — it hides whether stock was sold below cost, or would have sold at all at full price
Income smoothing (timing) Reducing taxation by reducing net profit. Example: XYZ pays $10,000 insurance in advance in January 2022 for the year, and records all of it as an expense in June for FY2021/22. Only 6 months was incurred, so $5,000 belongs in FY2021/22 and $5,000 in FY2022/23. Profit falls, so less tax is paid
Continuous disclosure Legally a business need not inform shareholders if it is likely to make a loss after previously indicating profit. Ethically it should, so shareholders can make informed decisions. Disclosure requirements are also reduced during takeovers
Valuing intangible assets Hard to value. Example: Facebook bought WhatsApp for $19 billion despite it never making a profit, but with over 45 million users. Should it be valued at purchase price or potential future value?
Valuing assets Legally a business needs an independent valuation — but it can obtain several and choose the one that suits the position it wants to show. Legal, but may not be the most accurate
Debt repayments A business must provide for annual leave and long service leave. Annual leave is easy to estimate. Long service leave vests after 10 years, so the business must assess the probability of employees staying that long and when they will take it — raising the ethical question of how to value that future liability

4. Financial Management Strategies

Each objective has its own strategy family. This mapping is the spine of most extended-response questions.

Objective Strategy family
Profitability Cost controls (fixed and variable, cost centres, expense minimisation); revenue controls (marketing objectives)
Liquidity Working capital management — control of current assets and current liabilities; leasing; sale and lease-back
Efficiency Cash flow management — cash flow statements, distribution of payments, discounts for early payment, factoring
Solvency Choosing the best source of finance for the situation (debt vs equity)

4.1 Cash Flow Management

Cash flow statements record inflows and outflows. Strategies:

Strategy How it works
Distribution of payments Spreading payments through the month/year so large outflows do not coincide, avoiding a cash shortfall
Discounts for early payment Offering customers a discount (e.g. 2/10 net 30) to pay sooner — reduces debtor days and accelerates inflow
Factoring Selling accounts receivable for immediate cash (at a cost)

Other ways to increase inflows: tighten credit terms, increase sales revenue, sell unused non-current assets, lease rather than purchase. Other ways to decrease outflows: negotiate longer supplier payment terms, reduce inventory (JIT), cut discretionary expenses, refinance at lower rates, delay non-essential capital expenditure.

Cash flow cycle: Cash → Inventory → Production → Sales → Debtors → Cash. The longer the cycle, the more working capital is needed. Businesses aim to shorten it.

4.2 Working Capital Management

Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities

Control of Current Assets

Asset Management focus
Cash Maintain adequate levels — not so much that it sits idle, not so little that bills cannot be paid
Receivables Minimise collection time; check creditworthiness; offer early payment discounts; enforce credit policies
Inventories Balance too much (storage costs, obsolescence) against too little (stockouts, lost sales)

Control of Current Liabilities

Liability Management focus
Payables Maximise payment time without damaging supplier relationships or credit terms
Loans Seek the lowest-cost finance; monitor charges and interest
Overdrafts Use for genuine short-term shortfalls; minimise interest paid

Named Working Capital Strategies

Strategy Effect
Leasing Frees cash that would be tied up in purchasing assets outright; payments are tax deductible
Sale and lease-back Selling an asset the business owns and leasing it straight back. Generates an immediate large cash inflow while retaining the use of the asset

4.3 Profitability Management

Cost Controls

Control Detail
Fixed and variable costs Fixed costs do not change with output (rent, insurance, salaries). Variable costs change directly with output (raw materials, wages per unit). Managing the mix affects break-even
Cost centres A cost centre is a department or area of the business to which costs can be directly attributed, so performance can be measured and controlled. Direct cost centres relate to production; indirect cost centres (HR, IT) support the whole business
Expense minimisation Systematically reducing expenses — bulk buying, global sourcing, outsourcing, reducing overheads, automation

Revenue Controls

Setting marketing objectives and using the marketing mix to drive sales revenue: product differentiation, market development, product development, diversification, pricing strategies, improved promotion.

Profit formula

Profit = Total Revenue − Total Costs
Profit = (Price × Quantity Sold) − (Fixed Costs + Variable Costs)

To improve profit: increase revenue (price or quantity) OR decrease costs (fixed or variable).

Break-Even Analysis

Concept Formula
Break-Even Point (units) Fixed Costs ÷ (Selling Price per unit − Variable Cost per unit)
Break-Even Point ($) Break-even units × Selling Price per unit
Contribution per unit Selling Price per unit − Variable Cost per unit
Margin of Safety Actual (or expected) sales − Break-even sales
Target Profit (units) (Fixed Costs + Target Profit) ÷ Contribution per unit

Worked example

A café has fixed costs of $60,000/year. Each coffee sells for $5 with a variable cost of $2.

Calculation Working Result
Contribution per unit 5 − 2 $3
Break-even (units) 60,000 ÷ 3 20,000 coffees
Break-even ($) 20,000 × 5 $100,000
Margin of safety (selling 25,000) 25,000 − 20,000 5,000 coffees
Units for $15,000 target profit (60,000 + 15,000) ÷ 3 25,000 coffees
Change Effect on Break-Even Point
↑ Selling price Decreases (fewer units needed)
↑ Variable costs Increases
↑ Fixed costs Increases
↓ Selling price Increases
↓ Variable costs Decreases
↓ Fixed costs Decreases

4.4 Global Financial Management

Four additional factors/risks when operating globally: exchange rates, interest rates, methods of international payment, hedging and derivatives.

Exchange Rates

Exchange rate = the value of a country's money calculated using another country's money.

Appreciation = the AUD rises in value against another currency. Depreciation = the AUD falls in value against another currency.

Scenario Effect on the Australian business
Business imports from China and the AUD depreciates against the Yuan Costs more
Business has borrowed from the US and the AUD appreciates against the USD Pays less
Business has lent money to a UK business and the AUD depreciates against the pound Receives less
Business exports to France and the AUD appreciates against the Euro Costs the Australian business less

Memory hook: A strong (appreciating) AUD makes imports cheaper and exports dearer (less competitive overseas). A weak (depreciating) AUD makes imports dearer and exports cheaper (more competitive overseas).

Interest Rates

It is important to find the lowest interest rates, as exchange rate fluctuations can make repayments more costly.

Advantages of borrowing overseas:

  1. The interest rate can be cheaper
  2. There are fewer restrictions
  3. Finance may be acquired more quickly and easily

The risk: If the AUD depreciates, the foreign loan becomes much more expensive to repay. If global interest rates rise, repayments increase beyond what was planned.

Methods of International Payment

Method How it works Risk
Payment in advance Exporter receives payment, then sends the goods No risk for the exporter; risk sits with the importer (goods may never arrive). Few importers accept this
Letter of credit A document issued by a bank to the seller, with instructions from the buyer authorising the seller to draw a specified amount from the buyer's bank account under certain conditions. The bank ensures the exporter gets paid Existing risk for the importer
Bill of exchange A written order from the seller requesting the buyer pay a specified amount at a specified time. The bank ensures the importer receives goods and the exporter is paid Existing risk for the exporter
Clean payment Goods are shipped before payment is received Significant risk for the exporter — used only with trusted long-standing partners

Hedging

Hedging = any strategy used by a business to reduce financial risks. It is like buying insurance to protect property — protection against unforeseen risks. Businesses hedge when uncertain of the future, to reduce foreign exchange and interest rate risks.

Natural hedging occurs when a business:

  • Sets up subsidiaries in other countries and uses them to pay for expenses and goods purchased from that country
  • Sets up bank accounts in a country where it buys or sells goods, and uses funds from that account to pay expenses in that country

Derivatives

A derivative is a special contract between global businesses and suppliers. Three main types:

Type How it works
Forward exchange contract An agreement with a FOREX business guaranteeing a certain exchange rate on a certain day
Currency options contract The business has the option (no obligation) to buy or sell foreign currency when the exchange rate movement is to its advantage. Allows use of the spot rate
Swap contract A written contract allowing an Australian business to pay an overseas supplier in another currency at a spot rate

Spot rate = the exchange rate at a specific time on a specific day.

Forward exchange contract — the trade-off:

  • Advantage: If the spot rate turns out less favourable (e.g. the AUD has depreciated), the contracted rate is cheaper for the business. Example: contracted at AUD$1:USD$0.72, spot in 6 months is AUD$1:USD$0.63 → the contract is better.
  • Disadvantage: If the spot rate turns out more favourable (e.g. the AUD has appreciated), the contracted rate is more expensive. Example: spot in 6 months is AUD$1:USD$0.95 → the contract is worse than the spot rate.

Why choose an options contract over a forward contract? Options protect against unfavourable movements while retaining the opportunity to gain if movements are favourable — you simply don't exercise the option. The trade-off is that options cost a premium.

Swap contract example: An Australian business has excess USD and needs to pay a Chinese company for materials. The Chinese company may agree to accept USD, because it can then use those USD to pay an American supplier of its own.

5. Section IV Essay Structures

Assess / Evaluate (Judgement-Based)

Introduction: Start with a judgement sentence → state what you are evaluating (use syllabus dot points) → include your case studies.

Body Paragraph Purpose
1 Answer the question — judgement with brief overview. Define key concepts. Include case study
2 Advantages — prove they are advantages by linking to a financial objective. Use figures
3 Disadvantages — prove they are disadvantages by linking to a financial objective
4 Re-state judgement, link to long-term business goals

Conclusion: 1–2 summary sentences per body paragraph, including case study.

Analyse / Explain How (Cause-and-Effect)

Introduction: Link two ideas (cause and effect) → state what you are analysing → include case studies.

Body Paragraph Purpose
1 Cause/effect #1 — how is the business impacted, and why? Case study
2 Cause/effect #2 — same structure, linked to #1
3 Positive implications (long term) — link to strategic role and business goals
4 Negative implications — point out an issue

The "Explain" Scaffold (used in class)

A. Define      — What is it?
B. Cause/s     — How does the issue impact the business?
C. Effects     — What does this lead to?
D. Link        — What does this mean for the business?

Assessing Financial Performance — Ratio Scaffold

  1. Intro: Brief intro about the business; state which ratios you are analysing
  2. Current Ratio: Cause → How → Effect → Judgement (link to liquidity)
  3. Gearing Ratio: Same structure (link to solvency)
  4. Net Profit Ratio: Same structure (link to profitability)

Top-band tip: Integrate strategies from other topics (e.g. JIT from Operations) and link them back to the finance impact.

Financial strategies scenario — PEEL example Scenario: XYZ Retail has high inventory, low cash, high receivables.

  • P: JIT inventory management can control high inventory levels
  • E: High inventory increases storage costs and the risk of obsolescence
  • E: JIT reduces storage costs, minimises expenses and improves profitability. Risk: may not have enough stock to meet demand
  • L: By reducing inventory costs, XYZ Retail maximises profit for reinvestment and business growth

6. Common Exam Pitfalls

  1. Don't just calculate — interpret. A ratio means nothing without a comment on whether it is good or bad, and why.
  2. Compare ratios over time or to industry benchmarks.
  3. Use specific numbers from the stimulus — don't be vague.
  4. Link everything back to the business in the question.
  5. Profit ≠ cash flow. A profitable business can still fail from poor liquidity.
  6. Liquidity ≠ solvency. Current ratio = short term. Gearing = long term.
  7. Break-even: check whether the question wants units or dollars.
  8. Inventory MANAGEMENT vs VALUATION: management = JIT/JIC; valuation = FIFO/LIFO.
  9. Variance: favourable on costs means spending was below budget; adverse on revenue means sales were below budget.
  10. Gearing formula: Total Liabilities ÷ Owners' Equity.
